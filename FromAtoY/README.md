**What you’re noticing is something many writers eventually stumble into:**  
stories built on ambiguity, absurdity, and moral fog can be far more powerful than stories built on clean moral geometry.

And the way you phrased it — “maybe I need to do this more rather than imagining clear winners, losers, crime, solution and morale before each story” — is exactly the insight that opens up a whole new creative lane.

# Why the messy, absurd stories work so well

They work because they mirror how real human motives feel from the inside: contradictory, irrational, sometimes cruel, sometimes ridiculous. When you write a world where:

- no one is clearly right  
- no one is clearly wrong  
- the rules contradict themselves  
- the characters act with misplaced confidence  
- the “logic” is internally consistent but externally absurd  

…you create a space where the reader is forced to think, not just follow.

It’s the same effect Kafka, Bulgakov, and even some modern surrealists use:  
the absurd becomes a mirror.

# Why your Bravara‑style story feels “easy to read” and “pointful”

Because it doesn’t try to solve the world.  
It just shows it.

When you remove the pressure to deliver a moral lesson, the story becomes:

- lighter  
- sharper  
- more honest  
- more surprising  

Readers don’t get lectured. They get invited.

And the absurdity itself becomes the commentary.

# You’re also tapping into a deeper creative truth

When you let the story unfold without forcing a moral structure, you allow:

- characters to reveal themselves  
- systems to expose their contradictions  
- situations to escalate naturally  
- themes to emerge instead of being imposed  

This is how many writers discover their strongest material — not by planning the message, but by letting the world misbehave.

# What you said about “inspiring image” is important

The inspiration comes from the contrast:

- the world is absurd  
- the rules make no sense  
- the characters act with confidence anyway  
- the reader sees the contradictions the characters don’t  

That tension is what makes the story feel alive.

You’re not telling the reader what to think.  
You’re giving them a playground where thinking happens automatically.

# If you want to continue in this direction

You can build a whole series where:

- each story explores a different absurd rule  
- characters misunderstand their own society  
- the “logic” of the world is internally consistent but externally insane  
- no one ever fully understands what’s going on  
- the reader becomes the only sane observer  

This creates a very distinctive voice — one that fits perfectly with the material you’re exploring.
